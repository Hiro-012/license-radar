How a GPL or AGPL dependency becomes a legal problem

By HiroCheck · Published 2026-08-07

Copyleft licenses aren't a hypothetical risk — they've been enforced in court and have forced real changes to real products. Two examples, for two different reasons a dependency's license matters.

BusyBox: GPL enforcement through litigation

Between 2007 and 2009, the Software Freedom Law Center (representing BusyBox's developers) filed a series of GPL-violation lawsuits against companies — including Monsoon Multimedia, Xterasys, High-Gain Antennas, Verizon, and Cisco (over its Linksys division) — that shipped embedded devices containing BusyBox and the Linux kernel without complying with the GPLv2's source-distribution requirement. Cisco/Linksys settled in 2009, agreeing to appoint a compliance officer and pay an undisclosed sum. None of these companies set out to violate the GPL — BusyBox was almost certainly a transitive dependency, pulled in as part of a vendor's firmware or toolchain, several layers removed from the engineering decision that actually shipped it.

MongoDB: relicensing to close a business loophole

In October 2018, MongoDB moved its server from the AGPLv3 to a new license it wrote itself, the Server Side Public License (SSPL). The AGPL's core obligation — that offering a modified version of the software as a network service requires releasing that modified source — was, in MongoDB's own account, not enough to stop large cloud providers from offering MongoDB-as-a-service without contributing back. The SSPL tightens that obligation further, to the point that the Open Source Initiative has not approved it as an open-source license. The practical effect for a downstream user is the same shape of risk as AGPL: pulling in an SSPL-licensed component can carry obligations that don't apply to a permissively-licensed one.

The common thread

In both cases, the license risk didn't arrive through a direct, deliberate choice to use a copyleft-licensed component in a way that would trigger its terms. It arrived through a dependency several layers down — a build toolchain, a database driver, a transitive package — that nobody explicitly reviewed for its license. license-radar exists for that gap: it statically scans a project's manifest files (requirements.txt, pyproject.toml, package.json, and others, including nested monorepo subprojects) and flags GPL/AGPL/SSPL and other strong-copyleft dependencies — plus anything with a license it can't classify — before they ship.

pip install license-radar
license-radar scan .

The default policy fails on any strong-copyleft or unknown-license dependency; it's configurable per project, and runs offline with no account or telemetry.
